# Student instructions: Pinpoint document-mining exercise

## Your role

You are helping a reporter quickly review a collection of public documents from the Prince George's County Board of License Commissioners.

Your job is to use Pinpoint to find one possible story lead, identify the source material behind it, and decide how much verification is still needed before the lead could be used in a story pitch.

## Time

Plan for about 20 minutes.

## Before you start

Open the Pinpoint collection and notice the main parts of the interface:

- The **Documents** list
- The search bar
- The **Gemini** panel on the right
- The source setting, such as **Entire collection**
- The Gemini options: **Find answers**, **Summarize**, **Compare**, **Extract data**, and **Label documents**

Pinpoint may warn that generative AI features can display inaccurate information, including about people. Treat that warning as part of the exercise: every useful output needs to be checked against the original documents.

## Step 1: Orient to the collection

Before searching or asking Gemini anything, look at the collection as a whole.

Answer:

- What documents are in this collection?
- Who created them?
- What time period do they cover?
- What kinds of questions could these documents help answer?
- What would these documents not be able to answer?

## Step 2: Use manual search first

Use the search bar to search for an establishment, organization, place, issue, rule number, license type, or hearing type.

Possible search terms:

- `violation`
- `transfer`
- `license`
- `beer`
- `wine`
- `Hyattsville`
- `College Park`
- `preliminary matters`

Record:

- The search term you used.
- Which documents mention it.
- Whether the mention is central or incidental.
- The exact passage, page, or document you would save.

Think about the risk level of this task. Search is mostly about finding source material, but you still need to read the document and inspect the context.

## Step 3: Use Gemini to summarize cautiously

Use **Summarize** on one document, selected documents, or the entire collection, depending on what your instructor asks you to do.

Write a cautious one- or two-sentence summary using wording such as:

> This document appears to concern...

Then answer:

- What did the summary capture accurately?
- What did it omit?
- Did it overstate anything?
- What exact source passage would you check before using the summary?

Think about the risk level of this task. Summaries are useful for triage, but they can omit context or make a document sound clearer than it is.

## Step 4: Use Gemini to extract data

Use **Extract data** to pull structured information from the documents.

Try a prompt like:

> Extract establishment name, licensee name, address, hearing date, matter type, alleged violation or issue, and source document. If a field is not available, write "not found." Clearly label allegations as allegations.

Record at least one row in the structured extraction table in the worksheet.

Think about:

- Which fields were clean?
- Which fields were ambiguous?
- Did the output clearly distinguish allegations, requests, decisions, and facts?
- Did it include source documents or passages?
- What would happen if an extraction error were repeated across many rows?

Think about the risk level of this task. Once extracted information becomes data, mistakes can spread into analysis.

## Step 5: Use Gemini to find answers

Use **Find answers** to ask one collection-level question.

Possible questions:

- What types of matters appear in these documents?
- Which establishments appear more than once?
- What kinds of alleged violations are mentioned?
- Which documents mention transfers or new licenses?

Record:

- The question you asked.
- The answer Pinpoint gave.
- Whether the answer appears complete.
- Which document or passage you would check first.

Think about the risk level of this task. A collection-level answer can sound authoritative even when it is incomplete or based on only some documents.

## Step 6: Identify a claim

Find one sentence or AI-generated statement that sounds like a factual claim.

Answer:

- What is the claim?
- Who is making the claim?
- What document supports that the claim was made?
- Does the document prove the claim is true?
- What source would verify it?

Think about the risk level of this task. A document can prove that someone said something. It may not prove that the thing is true.

## Step 7: Write one possible story angle

Use what you found to write one possible story angle. Phrase it as a question, not a conclusion.

Examples:

- Why did...?
- How often...?
- Who benefits from...?
- What changed after...?
- What explains...?

Then list:

- What evidence you have.
- What evidence you do not have.
- Who you would contact next.
- What data or documents you would need next.

Think about the risk level of this task. Story angles are leads for reporting, not findings.

## Optional: Label documents

If time allows, use **Label documents** to group the collection into document types such as:

- agenda
- minutes
- administrative voting session
- preliminary matters
- regular session

Ask yourself:

- Did the labels make sense?
- Did any documents get mislabeled?
- How could labels help organize reporting?

## Final check

Before you finish, make sure your worksheet separates:

- What the original document says.
- What Pinpoint or Gemini helped you find.
- What you inferred.
- What still needs verification.
